I've been working through the strategic positioning of LMaaS and wanted to share where the thinking has landed. Quick level-set on terms before I get into it.

LMaaS (Last Mile as a Service) is the model we're evaluating where MaiaEdge provides a platform — hardware and software — that sits inside regional fiber operators' metro ethernet networks. The hardware is the PBC (with the port extender as an optional addition), installed in the fiber operator's metro ethernet. The cloud-hosted software layer manages provisioning, cloud interconnect, multi-tenant path slicing, and the service lifecycle on top of it. The fiber operator owns the glass and the customer relationship. LMaaS turns their transport into managed connectivity capable of reaching cloud providers, colocation facilities, and neoclouds — and just as importantly, gives those destinations a managed path back to the enterprise. Think of it as embedding a private Equinix Fabric inside regional fiber networks. "Fiber operator" here means companies like Arvig, Consolidated, Ziply, TDS, and Shenandoah — local/regional operators that own last-mile fiber and sell DIA and on-net ethernet but don't offer carrier-grade cloud or connectivity services today.

The core problem: these fiber operators are structurally unable to sell cloud onramp, multi-cloud connectivity, or neocloud access on their own. They lack NOC/SOC capabilities, cloud provider integrations, multi-vendor orchestration, and the consultative sales motion these services require. Their organizations are built around physical infrastructure: fiber routes, poles, splitters, truck rolls. Meanwhile, BEAD and RDOF subsidies are consuming all available capital and management attention on fiber buildouts at 15-25x EBITDA multiples. The opportunity cost of investing in cloud service capabilities is irrational right now.

What they can sell successfully are turnkey vendor-managed platform products — and Calix is the proof point. Calix built a complete platform — GigaSpire hardware in the home paired with cloud software (CommandIQ, SmartHome, Revenue EDGE) — that lets fiber operators offer managed home WiFi, security, and parental controls without building any of the underlying technology. Same model as LMaaS: hardware in the network plus cloud orchestration on top. The operator never had to build a NOC or hire software engineers — and Calix built a $2B+ market cap business on this model. LMaaS is the Calix equivalent for enterprise cloud and neocloud connectivity.

The regulatory structure matters. Telecom-classified services carry roughly 31% in regulatory surcharges (USF, state excise taxes, 911 fees, gross receipts). LMaaS avoids this by using a Split Product Model: the fiber operator remains carrier of record and sells transport (E-Line, wavelength, dark fiber) with all associated telecom taxes and FCC compliance. MaiaEdge provides the platform — PBC hardware plus routing, orchestration, and cloud/neocloud interconnect enablement — as a separate information service. This is how Megaport, PacketFabric, and Equinix Fabric are structured. The fiber operator's compliance posture doesn't change at all.

The billing model is Port + Path. Fiber operator invoices transport (the physical port). MaiaEdge invoices fabric paths (logical connections to cloud, colocation, and neocloud destinations) as contractually distinct line items with separate tax treatment. Revenue sharing happens internally.

There's a distribution angle worth discussing. Rather than going operator-by-operator, Calix itself could be a strategic distribution partner. Calix has 1,600+ fiber operator customers and an established partner ecosystem with 250+ certified partners. Their SmartBiz and ManagedBiz platforms cover small and mid-market business WiFi and security, but there is no cloud onramp, neocloud connectivity, or SD-WAN capability anywhere in their portfolio. Their new Calix One platform, launched in February, is built as an AI-native enterprise framework on Google Cloud — architecturally designed for this kind of service integration. If LMaaS were embedded in the Calix platform the way SmartBiz is today, MaiaEdge would have a channel into 1,600 fiber operators overnight. A side benefit: Calix has already built billing system integration for fiber companies, which could simplify the Port + Path invoicing model significantly. Plume is a secondary option — 400+ ISP relationships, expanding into enterprise with WorkPass — but they're more WiFi-centric and don't have Calix's depth in fiber operator relationships.

LMaaS is also a critical piece of the neocloud story. Neoclouds need low-latency, dynamic capacity to the enterprise, and they don't have the last mile distribution to deliver it. The hyperscalers solved this through massive CapEx on direct connect infrastructure and peering. Neoclouds can't replicate that. LMaaS embedded inside regional fiber operators gives neoclouds a managed path from their compute to enterprise premises without building their own last mile. The same model works with companies like RevNet for Azure Local. The fiber operators get new revenue, the neoclouds and distributed AI providers get enterprise reach, and MaiaEdge is the orchestration layer that connects them.

On what this means for MaiaEdge as a company: LMaaS is a natural service extension of the existing PBC business. We're already in the metro ethernet. The platform just opens up new revenue streams on top of that installed base — usage-based fabric path fees and revenue shares alongside the existing hardware and subscription model. Every fiber operator on the platform makes it more valuable to every cloud, colocation, and neocloud destination, and that network effect compounds as we scale.

The net of it: LMaaS is a turnkey platform that fills the operational gap (no NOC or cloud integrations needed), the regulatory gap (fiber operator's compliance posture unchanged), and the commercial gap (billing, pricing, revenue share pre-structured). The pitch to fiber operators: participate in cloud and neocloud connectivity revenue without becoming a carrier or diverting from your buildout strategy.

Next steps: I'm already engaging fiber operators to discuss the Port + Path model and test whether the turnkey pitch resonates with their sales teams. On competitive positioning — Megaport, PacketFabric, and Equinix are not competitors. They all terminate at the data center or colocation facility and have no way to reach the enterprise across the last mile. LMaaS solves that problem for them. If we can get Equinix to endorse LMaaS as a reference architecture for last mile connectivity into their fabric, that becomes a powerful marketing asset that gives us credibility in every fiber operator conversation. We should also open an exploratory conversation with Calix's partner team and sketch out the contract and billing framework.
